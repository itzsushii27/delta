Why was this made? What does it do?

As you likely know if you stumbled unto this webpage, Elo is a rating system that's used in many popular games, such as chess, Rocket League, League of Legends, etc etc, with the job of giving players a score assigned to themselves, representing their skill level. Elo is indeed a well built system, but it has some flaws. For example: It's too complicated, is too harsh to some players and too kind to others, and it has far too many factors to be used in a basic calculator. I'm addressing all of these in my new system: Delta.

Then what's that big block?

The multiplier out front, K, is how reactive a rating is at any given moment. The raw piece inside it, max(8, (3100−r)/75), shrinks steadily as a rating climbs toward 2500, and flattens out at a floor of 8, so a rating never gets completely frozen at very high-level play. Below 2500 the term will change lower rated player's ratings more, and higher rated players less, and there are a few good reasons for this design choice. Firstly, lower rated players or far more likely to still be improving, hence when they play well, they should start to see rating improvements mirroring their skill. Secondly, experience. Top players have obviously been playing for far longer than everyone else, meaning there is more certainty of where they are placed, and erratic change contradicts that. There are more examples, but for the sake of consision, I will stop there.

The compression factor c is there for systems that wish not to use the dynamic K system I showed to the extent that's shown. A higher C means a lower difference between the rating change of say a 250 and a 2500. At c = 1 the term is untouched. Push c higher and every rating's K-factor gets pulled closer to 8, flattening the difference in volatility between a brand-new player and a seasoned one without moving the floor or the ceiling. If the people running the system dislike the rating based K, they can simply choose a static K value, like 20.

So what does the rest do?

The first piece of the bracket, , is the actual score rate: net wins over total games, shifted up by one so an even record lands on 1 instead of 0. It runs from 0 for an unbroken loss streak up to 2 for an unbroken win streak, regardless of how many games g that record is spread across.

The second piece, , is the expected version of that same rate, built from the same tanh curve Elo's own expected-score logistic reduces to, also shifted up by one so it sits on the same 0–2 scale as the performance rate above. It reads 1 for an even matchup, climbs toward 2 the more you're favored, and drops toward 0 the more you're the underdog.

The rating change comes from the gap between the two — performance rate − expected rate — which stays within ±1 of either extreme no matter how large g gets. A long, mostly-drawn stretch against a similarly-rated opponent now correctly produces a rating change near zero, instead of one that grows with the game count.

Where this leaves things

None of this claims to be a finished, validated rating system — it's one attempt at fixing the two things about Elo that bothered me most, while keeping the one piece that's earned its keep over decades of use. Everything above the expected-score curve — the K-factor shape, the floor of 8, the ceiling of 3100, the square-root scaling of performance — is a deliberate design choice, and open to being wrong in ways I haven't found yet.
